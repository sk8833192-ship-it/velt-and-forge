Food-Safe Ice Stamps Without Lead: What to Ask Before You Buy

An ice stamp presses a metal face into ice that ends up in a drink. Many brass stamps are cut from free-machining C36000, which contains 2.5–3.7% lead. Here are the exact numbers, a four-question verification checklist, and the two lead-free routes: bismuth eco-brass and 304 stainless steel.

Quick Answer & Engineering Benchmarks

Quick Answer and Engineering Benchmarks

  1. Short answer: for a food-contact ice stamp, choose a lead-free alloy (bismuth eco-brass) or 304 stainless steel, and get the exact alloy grade in writing.
  2. Leaded brass C36000 contains 2.5–3.7% lead, which is 25,000–37,000 ppm.
  3. The NSF/ANSI 372 lead-free threshold is 0.25% weighted average lead, which is 2,500 ppm. We use it as a benchmark, not as a certification claim.
  4. Our target bismuth lead-free eco-brass measured about 0.0022% lead (≈ 22 ppm) in an optical-spectrometer reading of a supplier alloy sample.
  5. 304 stainless steel is a no-lead-added alternative: ask for the mill certificate. It costs more and needs a slightly longer contact time on ice.

The risk in one paragraph

Why the alloy grade matters on ice

Brass is copper and zinc. To make it easy to machine, many shops use C36000, a free-cutting brass with lead added so the chips break cleanly. It is an excellent engraving metal and a common stock choice for stamps, which is exactly why the question matters: a stamp seller who does not name the alloy may be using it.

Ice stamps touch wet, cold ice for a few seconds and the cube then goes into a drink. We would rather remove lead from the question than argue about exposure, so our target alloy is a bismuth lead-free eco-brass, with 304 stainless steel as the second option.

The lead numbers, side by side

Percent and parts-per-million are the same measurement written two ways: 1% is 10,000 ppm. The table in the quick answer is the short version; here is the arithmetic.

  • Bismuth eco-brass: ~0.0022% × 10,000 = ≈ 22 ppm.
  • NSF/ANSI 372 threshold: 0.25% × 10,000 = 2,500 ppm, so the sample reading is about 2,500 ÷ 22 ≈ 113 times lower.
  • C36000: 2.5–3.7% × 10,000 = 25,000–37,000 ppm, roughly 10 to 15 times the threshold and more than 1,000 times the sample reading.
Read this carefully

The ≈ 22 ppm figure comes from an optical-spectrometer reading of a supplier alloy sample. It is not a production batch result and not a certification. Our process is to spectrometer-check each production batch and publish the sheet before launch. For the longer metallurgy discussion see Lead-Free Brass, C36000, and What to Ask Before You Buy.

The 4-question buyer checklist

Ask any ice stamp seller these four questions before you pay. A good seller answers in a sentence. Silence, vague wording or a link to a generic “safe materials” page is information too.

Brass or 304 steel: which lead-free route?

Both avoid leaded brass. Pick on how you stamp, not on lead.

Quick questions, straight answers

Is a brass ice stamp food safe?

It depends on the alloy. Leaded free-machining brass C36000 contains 2.5–3.7% lead (25,000–37,000 ppm). A bismuth lead-free eco-brass or 304 stainless steel avoids that. Ask any seller for the exact alloy grade in writing.

What is C36000 brass?

C36000 is a free-machining brass with about 2.5–3.7% lead added so it cuts cleanly. It is common in stamps and fittings, which is why asking for the alloy grade matters.

What does 22 ppm lead mean?

It means about 0.0022% lead by weight. Our target bismuth eco-brass read about 22 ppm in an optical-spectrometer reading of a supplier alloy sample, versus the 2,500 ppm (0.25%) NSF/ANSI 372 benchmark.

Does 304 stainless steel contain lead?

Lead is not part of the 304 grade. Ask the seller for the mill certificate to confirm the specific product.
